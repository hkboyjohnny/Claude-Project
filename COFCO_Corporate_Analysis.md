# COFCO Corporation (中粮集团): China's food security champion, three engines, three different cycles

*Corporate analysis, written in the style of a sell-side coverage note. Prepared 13 October 2026. Basis: company filings and public reporting through H1 2026. COFCO's parent company is unlisted and does not publish profit by segment, so each sector is read through the group entity that does report: COFCO International and the domestic processing platforms (grains and oilseeds), COFCO Joycome (pork), and China Mengniu Dairy (dairy). This is an educational analysis, not investment advice, and it is not issued by any bank.*

---

## 1. Key takeaways (read this if nothing else)

1. **What COFCO is.** COFCO is China's largest state-owned food and agriculture company. It buys grain and oilseeds around the world, ships them to China, processes them into cooking oil, animal feed, flour and rice, and sells branded foods. Its job is partly commercial (make money) and partly national (keep China fed at stable prices). That dual role explains a lot of its behaviour.

2. **The three businesses that matter most:**
   - **Grains and oilseeds (trading and processing)** is the core: the largest by revenue and the most strategic. Profits come from *margins between* prices, not from price levels.
   - **Pork (COFCO Joycome)** is the most volatile: it rides China's "hog cycle" and swung back into heavy losses in H1 2026.
   - **Dairy (Mengniu, which COFCO effectively controls as largest shareholder)** is the consumer brand: it is a steadier business, but it is in a multi-year slump caused by weak demand and too much milk.

3. **Why earnings fluctuate.** The three businesses are each tied to a different cycle: global commodity spreads, the Chinese hog price, and Chinese consumer demand plus the raw-milk cycle. In 2025 and 2026 all three were in a weak phase at the same time. That is unusual, and it is why group earnings look soft right now.

4. **Our view.** The business franchise is strong and the state backing is clear: COFCO is a national food security platform and has a government policy mandate. Near-term earnings are cyclically depressed. We expect pork to recover first, as the government-led cut in sow numbers works through. Grain margins should be next, helped by more trade flows after the 2025 US-China truce. Dairy recovers last, because it depends on consumers.

## 2. COFCO in plain language

### 2.1 What does the company actually do?

Think of COFCO as a giant pipeline for food:

```
  Farms (Brazil, Argentina, US, Black Sea, China)
          │   buy, store, ship  ← COFCO International / domestic grain
          ▼
  Ports & processing plants in China
          │   crush soybeans → oil + meal; mill wheat & rice; corn → ethanol, starch
          ▼
  Two kinds of customer
     ├── Farmers & feed mills (soybean meal feeds pigs and chickens) ──► Pork (Joycome)
     └── Households & restaurants (Fortune 福临门 oil, rice, flour, Mengniu milk, sugar, wine)
```

The pipeline matters for analysis because **the businesses are connected**. Soybean meal from the crushing plants is the main protein in pig feed. When pig farmers lose money and cut their herds, demand for soybean meal falls, and crushing margins suffer too. A shock at one end of the pipeline travels along it.

### 2.2 Ownership and corporate structure

- **Owner:** 100% owned by the Chinese central government through SASAC, the body that owns China's large central SOEs. Since 2017, COFCO has been run as a pilot "state capital investment company". This means the parent behaves like an active owner of a portfolio of businesses, not as a single operating company.
- **Listed subsidiaries** (these are what investors can actually buy): China Mengniu Dairy (HK), COFCO Joycome (HK), China Foods (HK), COFCO Sugar (Shanghai), COFCO Technology & Industry (Shenzhen), COFCO Capital (Shenzhen), and the agricultural processing platforms. **COFCO International**, the overseas trading arm, is unlisted but publishes annual figures.
- **Size:** COFCO ranks in the Fortune Global 500 and its revenue is in the same league as the global "ABCD" grain traders (ADM, Bunge, Cargill, Louis Dreyfus). Its profit margin is thinner than theirs, because part of its mission is price stability rather than profit.

### 2.3 Why the state mandate matters to an investor

COFCO is expected to **buy when others will not**, and sometimes **sell when others will not**. Examples:
- In late 2025, COFCO and the state reserve agency Sinograin were essentially the only Chinese buyers of US soybeans after the US-China trade truce, while private crushers bought cheaper Brazilian and Argentine beans.
- In pork and grain, COFCO helps the state with stockpiling and releasing reserves to smooth prices.

**The upside:** implicit government support, cheap funding, and privileged access to import quotas and port infrastructure. **The downside:** some trades are done for policy reasons, not for maximum profit. Treat COFCO's earnings as "commercial earnings minus a national-service cost."

## 3. Sector 1: Grains and oilseeds (trading and processing). The core engine

### 3.1 How the business makes money

There are two halves:

| Half | What it does | How it earns |
|---|---|---|
| **Origination and trading** (mainly COFCO International, HQ Geneva) | Buys soybeans, corn, wheat, sugar, coffee and cotton from farmers in South America, the Black Sea, Australia and elsewhere. It stores them in silos, moves them through its own ports and ships them, largely to China. | It earns a **spread**: the difference between the price paid at the farm gate and the price received at the destination, minus freight and storage. When prices are volatile or trade routes are disrupted, spreads widen and traders earn more. |
| **Domestic processing** (oilseed crushing, flour and rice milling, corn processing) | Turns raw grain into products: soybeans into soybean oil (for cooking) and soybean meal (for animal feed), wheat into flour, corn into starch and ethanol. | It earns a **processing margin** ("crush margin"): the value of oil plus meal, minus the cost of the beans. This is again a spread, not a price level. |

**The single most important idea for a new reader:** COFCO's grain business does *not* simply make more money when grain prices are high. It makes money when the **gaps** between prices are wide: across countries, across time, and between raw input and processed output. High but stable prices can mean thin margins. Volatile prices can mean fat ones.

### 3.2 Competitive position

- **Scale in China:** COFCO is one of the two dominant edible-oil and crushing groups in China. The other is Yihai Kerry (Arawana brand), which is controlled by Singapore's Wilmar. COFCO's consumer oil brand is **Fortune (福临门)**.
- **Global reach:** COFCO International was built largely through two acquisitions in 2014: Nidera (Dutch) and Noble Agri (part of Hong Kong's Noble Group). It handles third-party volumes of more than 100 million tonnes a year, which puts it in the top tier of global traders. Brazil is its key origin. It ships about 17–18 million tonnes of soybeans and corn a year out of Brazil, roughly 80% to China, and it is building a large new export terminal at the Port of Santos.
- **Moat:** Owning silos, ports and ships in the countries that grow the crop is hard to copy. This kind of infrastructure is what separates a real trader from a paper trader.

### 3.3 Operational performance: the story since 2021

**Phase 1: boom (2021–2022).** COVID supply-chain disruption, China rebuilding its pig herd (so strong feed demand), and then the Russia-Ukraine war created extreme price volatility and very wide spreads. Every major grain trader posted record or near-record profits in this period, and COFCO International did too.

**Phase 2: normalisation (2023–2024).** Supply chains healed and record South American harvests arrived, so prices fell and became calmer. Spreads compressed. COFCO International's revenue fell from about US$50bn in 2023 to about US$38.5bn in 2024, and third-party volumes fell from roughly 122 to 108 million tonnes. Lower revenue partly reflects lower *prices* rather than lower *activity*. Still, the drop in volume shows a tougher, more competitive market in which the company chose not to chase low-margin business.

**Phase 3: political disruption (2025 to now).** In 2025, US-China trade tensions caused China to stop buying US soybeans for much of the year and to switch almost entirely to Brazil. The effects were mixed:
- **Positive:** COFCO's heavy investment in Brazil (ports, origination) paid off. China's soybean imports hit a record in 2025, driven by Brazilian beans, and COFCO was well placed. In November 2025 it also signed purchase agreements for nearly 20 million tonnes of Brazilian soybeans, soybean oil and palm oil, worth more than US$10bn, with the major global traders.
- **Negative:** Domestic crushing margins were weak. Pig farmers were cutting herds (see Sector 2), so demand for soybean meal was soft, while record imports kept China well supplied. When the main by-product (meal) is cheap, the crush margin is squeezed.
- **Policy cost:** After the late-2025 US-China truce, China committed to buy 12 million tonnes of US soybeans in the short term and at least 25 million tonnes a year from 2026 to 2028. COFCO and Sinograin did most of that buying, even when US beans were more expensive than South American ones. This is the national-service cost from Section 2.3 in practice.

### 3.4 Why it fluctuates: the driver map

| Driver | Direction of effect on COFCO | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Price **volatility** (weather shocks, wars, trade disputes) | ↑ helps trading | Wider spreads and more arbitrage opportunities |
| Large, stable global harvests | ↓ hurts trading | Calm markets, thin spreads |
| China's hog herd size | ↑ herd = ↑ meal demand = ↑ crush margin | Soybean meal is the main pig-feed protein |
| Policy-directed purchases (e.g. US beans) | ↓ margin | Buys at above-market cost for strategic reasons |
| Freight and FX (US$/BRL, US$/RMB) | Either way | Changes landed cost and the timing of trades |
| Hedging and inventory valuation | Adds noise | Mark-to-market gains and losses can move reported profit between periods |

### 3.5 Outlook

We expect **modest improvement in 2027**. As the pig herd stabilises, meal demand should firm. The US-China purchase framework adds volume, albeit at lower margins. Weather and geopolitical shocks remain the main sources of upside, because they widen spreads. The structural trend is positive: China wants more of its food imports controlled by a Chinese company end to end, and COFCO is the designated vehicle.

## 4. Sector 2: Pork (COFCO Joycome). The cyclical swing factor

### 4.1 How the business makes money

COFCO Joycome (中粮家佳康) is an integrated pork producer:
1. **Hog farming:** breeds sows, raises piglets and grows them to market weight (about 120 kg).
2. **Slaughtering and fresh pork:** processes hogs into chilled, branded fresh pork for supermarkets and restaurants.
3. **Meat import trading:** imports frozen pork and beef.

**How profit works:** profit per pig equals **hog selling price minus cost to raise it**. Cost is mostly feed (corn and soybean meal), plus piglets, labour and disease control. Cost changes slowly. Price swings wildly. So nearly all the profit volatility comes from **price**.

### 4.2 Understanding the hog cycle (the key to this sector)

China eats roughly half the world's pork, and pork prices in China move in a well-known cycle that typically lasts **3–4 years**:

```
 High prices → farmers profitable → they keep more sows (mother pigs)
     ↑                                         ↓  (~10 months later)
 Herd shrinks ← farmers lose money ← more piglets → oversupply → prices fall
```

Biology creates the lag. A sow kept today produces pigs that reach market roughly 10 months later. Farmers therefore always react to *today's* price, but the supply effect arrives almost a year later. That lag guarantees overshooting in both directions.

### 4.3 Operational performance: the story since 2020

| Period | Hog price environment | Joycome outcome | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2019–2020 | Record highs (African Swine Fever killed a large share of China's herd) | Very strong profits | Scarcity pushed prices to historic peaks |
| 2021–2022 | Collapse, then partial rebound | Swings between loss and profit | The whole industry rebuilt herds too fast, then oversupply |
| 2023 | Prolonged low prices | Heavy losses | The sow herd was still too large and pork demand was sluggish |
| 2024 | Recovery from mid-year | Back to profit | Industry losses in 2023 forced sow cuts, so prices rose |
| H1 2025 | Moderate | Profit of about RMB 0.2bn (before biological asset adjustment) | Lower feed costs and better farm efficiency |
| H2 2025 | Prices slid to multi-year lows (about RMB 11/kg by December) | Full year 2025 swung back to a loss, even though revenue grew about 14% | Herds had been rebuilt again and supply exceeded demand |
| **H1 2026** | Prices at multi-year lows | **Loss of about RMB 1.1bn** before biological asset adjustment (about RMB 1.55bn after), revenue down about 2.5% | Low prices, plus deliberate scaling back of the import business |

**Two accounting points a new reader should know:**
- **"Before / after biological asset fair value adjustment."** Pig farmers must value their *live* pigs at market price on the balance sheet. When prices fall, the value of every pig in the barn is written down, which adds an extra paper loss. Analysts focus on the "before adjustment" number because it reflects the cash economics of the farm.
- **Revenue can rise while profit falls.** In 2025 Joycome sold more pigs (volume up), but each pig earned less or lost money. Volume growth did not offset the price fall.

### 4.4 What COFCO Joycome controls, and what it does not

- **Outside its control:** the national hog price. Joycome is a significant producer, but it is far smaller than the leaders (Muyuan, Wens), so it is a *price taker*.
- **Within its control:**
  - **Cost per kg:** feed formulation, breeding genetics, disease biosecurity and farm utilisation. Lower cost is the only lasting competitive advantage in this industry. Joycome has improved on this, but it is not a cost leader.
  - **Mix:** branded chilled fresh pork earns a steadier margin than live hogs. Joycome's push into branded fresh meat partly hedges the cycle.
  - **Discipline:** not over-expanding at cycle peaks.

### 4.5 Outlook: why we expect pork to lead the recovery

From mid-2025 the Chinese government pushed large producers to **cut sow herds and lower slaughter weights**. This was part of a wider campaign against "involution", meaning destructive over-competition. Because of the 10-month lag, those cuts should feed through to **tighter supply and better prices from late 2026 into 2027**. Our base case is that Joycome's losses narrow in H2 2026 and that it returns to profit in 2027. The risk is that smaller farmers do not follow the cuts. This has happened in past cycles and would delay the recovery.

## 5. Sector 3: Dairy (China Mengniu). The consumer franchise under pressure

### 5.1 How the business makes money

China Mengniu Dairy (蒙牛乳业) is one of China's two dairy leaders; the other is Yili. COFCO, through its dairy holding vehicle, is the **largest shareholder** and has the right to appoint key management.

Mengniu's product groups:
- **Liquid milk** (the bulk of sales): plain milk (Telunsu 特仑苏 premium brand), yogurt and milk drinks.
- **Ice cream**, **milk powder** (including infant formula, e.g. Bellamy's from Australia, Yashili), and **cheese** (through Milkground 妙可蓝多).
- **Upstream exposure:** stakes in large dairy-farm operators, such as Modern Dairy and China Shengmu.

**How profit works:** revenue (volume × price, driven by brand strength) minus raw milk cost minus heavy marketing and distribution spend. Dairy is a **branded consumer** business, so profit depends on consumer demand and pricing power, not commodity spreads.

### 5.2 Operational performance: the story since 2021

**2021–2022: growth.** Revenue climbed steadily, driven by health awareness during COVID and premiumisation (consumers trading up to Telunsu-type premium milk).

**2023–2025: a three-year slump.** Revenue fell for consecutive years. In 2025 it was about RMB 82bn, **down about 7%** year on year, with volume down about 4% and the rest from lower prices. Reported net profit in 2025 bounced sharply from a very depressed 2024 (when large impairments, including on Bellamy's, hit the accounts). Underlying profit, however, continued to decline. The gross margin held around 40%, helped by cheaper raw milk, and operating cash flow stayed healthy at about RMB 8.75bn.

### 5.3 Why it fluctuates: four overlapping causes

1. **Weak consumer demand.** Chinese households have been cautious since the property downturn. Premium milk is an easy item to trade down on, so consumers bought cheaper products or bought less.
2. **Too much milk (the raw-milk cycle).** In 2020–2022 high milk prices encouraged farms to expand herds aggressively. By 2023–2024 China produced more raw milk than consumers wanted. The effects ran in two directions:
   - *Good for Mengniu's costs:* raw milk got cheaper, which supported gross margin.
   - *Bad for Mengniu's pricing:* surplus milk was turned into cheap products and sold at a discount across the industry, which led to price wars and heavy promotions.
   - *Bad for Mengniu's associates:* its dairy-farm affiliates lost money, partly through write-downs on the value of dairy cows (the same biological-asset accounting as in pork). This flowed through into Mengniu's results as losses from associates.
3. **Structural demographics.** China's birth rate has fallen sharply, which shrinks the infant formula market. This is the main reason for the impairments on Mengniu's formula businesses.
4. **Competition.** Yili has outperformed Mengniu on several measures in recent years. Meanwhile, low-price channels such as community group-buying, discount stores and livestream e-commerce put pressure on branded pricing.

### 5.4 What management is doing

- **Price discipline over volume:** reducing distributor inventory and cutting back loss-making promotions.
- **Cost and efficiency programmes:** using cheaper raw milk to protect margins.
- **New growth areas:** cheese, B2B dairy ingredients for bakeries and restaurants (to absorb surplus milk at better value than milk powder), functional and health-oriented products, and overseas markets in Southeast Asia.
- **Clean-up of the balance sheet:** writing down the acquired formula businesses so that future earnings are cleaner.

### 5.5 Outlook

Dairy should recover **last** of the three. China's dairy-cow herd has been shrinking since 2024, and milk supply and demand should rebalance through 2026–2027. That will ease price wars, though it will also end the cheap-milk tailwind to cost. A real top-line recovery needs Chinese consumer confidence to return. That is a macro question beyond COFCO's control. We see 2026 as a stabilisation year rather than a growth year.

## 6. Putting it together: why group earnings move the way they do

### 6.1 The three cycles side by side

| | Grains and oilseeds | Pork | Dairy |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nature of business** | Commodity spread / processing margin | Commodity price minus farm cost | Branded consumer goods |
| **Main earnings driver** | Volatility, trade flows, crush margin | National hog price | Consumer demand and pricing power |
| **Typical cycle length** | Irregular (event-driven) | 3–4 years | Multi-year (herd cycle plus consumer cycle) |
| **Where we are now (late 2026)** | Weak margins, improving volumes | Trough, with recovery expected in 2027 | Bottoming, recovery slow |
| **COFCO's control over outcome** | Medium (assets, network) | Low (price taker) | Medium (brand, cost) |
| **Strategic importance to the state** | Very high | High (pork prices feed into CPI) | Medium |

### 6.2 The linkages that amplify swings

- **Pork and grains are linked through feed.** A bad hog cycle weakens meal demand and squeezes crushing margins. 2025–2026 showed this: both sectors were weak together.
- **Dairy and grains are linked through feed too.** Cows eat corn and soybean meal, so cheaper feed helps dairy farms. But surplus milk hurts dairy pricing, so the net effect on Mengniu is mixed.
- **All three are linked through the Chinese consumer.** Weak household confidence reduces both premium dairy and pork consumption.

**This is why 2025–2026 looks so soft:** it is rare for all three cycles to sit near their lows at the same time. In earlier years one business usually offset another. In 2022, for example, grain trading boomed while pork was weak. In 2024, pork recovered while dairy weakened.

### 6.3 Other businesses (briefly, for completeness)

- **Sugar (COFCO Sugar):** China's largest sugar importer and processor. Earnings fell in 2025 as global sugar prices dropped from their highs.
- **Corn biochemicals (COFCO Technology):** fuel ethanol, starch and citric acid, which are sensitive to corn and oil prices.
- **Branded foods and beverages (China Foods):** Coca-Cola bottling in parts of China, Great Wall wine and other packaged foods.
- **Financial services (COFCO Capital):** trust, futures and insurance brokerage. Its 2025 earnings were dragged down by market volatility.
- **Property:** COFCO has been reducing its exposure to non-core property in line with SASAC's push for SOEs to focus on their main business.

## 7. Analyst view and key risks

### 7.1 Overall view: strong franchise, cyclical trough, recovery staggered

- **Credit and franchise quality: strong.** COFCO is the state's designated food-security platform. It owns irreplaceable logistics infrastructure at home and abroad and benefits from implicit sovereign support. We see little risk to the group's standing.
- **Earnings: at or near a cyclical trough.** We expect a staggered recovery: **pork first (2027), grains second (2027, gradual), dairy last (2027–2028).**
- **For equity investors in the listed subsidiaries:** Joycome is the highest-beta recovery play, a direct bet on the hog price. Mengniu is a valuation-driven call on Chinese consumer recovery. The grain business is best accessed indirectly, since COFCO International is unlisted.

### 7.2 Key risks to the view

| Risk | What would happen |
|---|---|
| Hog capacity cuts fail (small farmers keep expanding) | Pork recovery delayed into 2028; meal demand stays weak |
| Renewed US-China trade conflict | More policy-driven buying and route disruption; outcome mixed for trading, negative for margins |
| Poor South American harvest / weather shock | Higher volatility (good for trading) but higher feed cost (bad for pork and dairy farms) |
| Chinese consumer confidence stays weak | Dairy and branded-food recovery slips further |
| Disease outbreaks (African Swine Fever, avian flu) | Short-term losses for affected herds; can later *raise* prices for survivors |
| Policy mandate expands | More "national service" trades that dilute commercial returns |
| Deforestation and ESG regulation (e.g. EU rules on soy and beef sourcing) | Higher compliance cost in Brazil; COFCO reports that 99% of its Brazilian soy was verified deforestation-free in 2024 |

### 7.3 What to watch (simple dashboard for a new reader)

1. **China's sow herd and hog price** (published monthly by the Ministry of Agriculture): the leading indicator for Joycome and for soybean meal demand.
2. **China soybean crush margin** (widely reported weekly): the processing profit for the grain business.
3. **China raw-milk price and dairy-cow herd**: the signal for when dairy price wars ease.
4. **US-China trade headlines and soybean purchase pace**: the policy overlay on COFCO International.
5. **Chinese retail sales and consumer confidence**: the demand backdrop for dairy and branded foods.

## Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **SOE / SASAC** | State-owned enterprise / the government body that owns China's central SOEs |
| **Spread** | The difference between two prices (e.g. buy in Brazil vs sell in China); a trader's profit source |
| **Crush margin** | Value of soybean oil plus soybean meal minus cost of the soybeans |
| **Soybean meal** | High-protein by-product of crushing soybeans; the main protein in pig and poultry feed |
| **Hog cycle** | Repeating 3–4 year boom-bust in pork prices caused by the lag between breeding decisions and supply |
| **Sow** | A breeding female pig; the number of sows determines future pork supply |
| **Biological asset fair value adjustment** | Accounting rule requiring live animals to be valued at market prices; creates paper gains or losses |
| **Associates** | Companies partly owned (not controlled); their profit or loss appears as one line in the owner's accounts |
| **Impairment** | A write-down when an asset (e.g. an acquired business) is worth less than its book value |
| **Involution (内卷)** | Chinese term for destructive over-competition; recent policy campaigns aim to curb it |
